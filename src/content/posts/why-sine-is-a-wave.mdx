Most of us meet sine as a ratio of two sides of a right triangle:

$$
\sin\theta = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}
$$

That definition is fine for solving triangles, and useless for explaining why
the graph of $\sin\theta$ is a smooth, endlessly repeating wave. Nothing about
"a ratio of two lengths" suggests oscillation. The wave shows up the moment you
stop thinking about triangles and start thinking about a circle.

## The setup

Take a circle of radius 1 centred at the origin, and a point walking
anticlockwise around it. Let $\theta$ be the angle the point has swept out from
the positive $x$-axis.[^radians] The point's coordinates are exactly

$$
(x, y) = (\cos\theta,\ \sin\theta)
$$

So $\sin\theta$ isn't fundamentally a ratio — it's the **height** of a point
travelling around a circle. The triangle definition is what you get by drawing
the point's vertical drop to the $x$-axis and noticing the hypotenuse has
length 1.

The curve on the right isn't a new object. It's the same height, replotted with

circle. Sine is what a circle looks like when you unroll it.

## Three things that stop being arbitrary

Once you see sine as circular height, a handful of facts you probably memorised
become unavoidable.

**It repeats with period $2\pi$.** Walking $2\pi$ radians takes the point all
the way around, back to where it started. Same position, same height, so
$\sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta$. The periodicity isn't a property of the
wave; it's a property of the loop.

**It's bounded by $\pm 1$.** The point never leaves a circle of radius 1, so
its height can't exceed 1. There is no $\theta$ with $\sin\theta = 1.4$, and
you don't need to inspect the graph to know it.

**$\sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.** This identity is the single most
over-memorised fact in trigonometry, and it is just Pythagoras applied to the
two legs in the figure. The legs have lengths $|\cos\theta|$ and
$|\sin\theta|$, the hypotenuse is the radius, and the radius is 1.

Watch the two legs as you drag: as the horizontal one shortens, the vertical
one lengthens to compensate, and it compensates in exactly the way that keeps
the sum of squares pinned at 1.

## Where the derivative comes from

Here's the part I found genuinely surprising the first time it clicked.

If the point moves around the circle at unit speed, its velocity is tangent to
the circle — perpendicular to the radius. Rotating the radius vector
$(\cos\theta, \sin\theta)$ by a quarter turn anticlockwise gives
$(-\sin\theta, \cos\theta)$, and that's the velocity.

The *vertical* component of that velocity is $\cos\theta$. But the vertical
position is $\sin\theta$, so the rate of change of $\sin\theta$ is $\cos\theta$:

$$
\frac{d}{d\theta}\sin\theta = \cos\theta
$$

No limits, no squeeze theorem, no table of values. The derivative of sine is
cosine because turning a vector 90° turns its $y$-component into the old
$x$-component. In the figure, this is why the traced wave is steepest exactly
when the horizontal leg is longest, and momentarily flat exactly when the point
is at the top or bottom of the circle.

## Doing it in code

If you want the wave without calling `Math.sin`, you can get it from rotation
alone. Repeatedly applying a small rotation matrix to a starting vector walks a
point around the circle, and its $y$-coordinate *is* the sine wave:

```js
// Rotate (1, 0) by a small angle, over and over.
// The y-coordinates that fall out trace a sine wave.
function circleWalk(steps = 64) {
  const d = (2 * Math.PI) / steps;
  const [c, s] = [Math.cos(d), Math.sin(d)];

  let x = 1;
  let y = 0;
  const heights = [];

  for (let i = 0; i < steps; i++) {
    heights.push(y);
    [x, y] = [c * x - s * y, s * x + c * y]; // one small rotation
  }

  return heights;
}
```

That `[x, y] = [c*x - s*y, s*x + c*y]` line is the whole of circular motion. It
is also, not coincidentally, how a great many oscillating systems get simulated:
springs, pendulums, LC circuits, and anything else whose state vector rotates
through a phase space. They all produce sine waves for the same structural
reason this circle does.

Which is, I think, the real reason sine turns up everywhere. It isn't that
nature is fond of triangles. It's that nature is full of things going round and
round, and sine is the shadow they cast.

[^radians]:
    Radians are what make this work cleanly. One radian is the angle that
    subtends an arc of length 1 on a unit circle, so the angle swept and the
    distance walked along the rim are the *same number*. Use degrees and that
    correspondence breaks, which is why the derivative identity above would
    pick up a stray factor of $\pi/180$.
