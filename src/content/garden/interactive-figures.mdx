A running list of what separates a figure that teaches from one that just
moves. Provisional, and I expect to disagree with some of this later.

**The reader should control the independent variable, not the clock.** An
animation on a loop makes you wait for the frame you want. A slider lets you
hold the interesting case still and stare at it. Autoplay is fine as an
invitation — it shows the thing is alive — but it should yield the moment
someone touches it.

**One figure, one claim.** If I need two sentences to say what a figure shows,
it's two figures. The unit-circle one in
[Why sine looks like a wave](/posts/why-sine-is-a-wave/) is close to the limit:
it carries the circle-to-wave correspondence, and I had to resist bolting the
derivative onto it as well.

**The degenerate cases have to be reachable.** A figure you can't drag to
$\theta = 0$ is hiding the case where the reader's intuition most likely
breaks. Clamping a slider to the pretty range is a way of avoiding the
question.

**Numbers alongside the picture.** A readout turns "roughly a third of the way
up" into $0.342$, which is the difference between a vague impression and
something you can check against your own arithmetic.

Still unsure about: whether to show the generating code next to every figure.
It's honest and it teaches a second thing for free, but it doubles the length
of the post and most readers don't want it.
